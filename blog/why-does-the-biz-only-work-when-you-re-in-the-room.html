Why does the biz only work when you're in the room?

Three years ago, a playground and I had a disagreement. The playground won.

We were halfway through an eight-hour drive to the Outer Banks, stopped for dinner, letting the girls run around. Next thing I knew, I was in an ER getting a brace, then spent the whole beach week on crutches while poor Skyler hauled the chairs, the coolers, the umbrellas, a three-year-old, an eight-month-old, and me back and forth across the sand every day.

Not my finest trip. But being stuck like that got me thinking about something I see in a lot of the businesses I work with. Went back through a few things I wrote this past month and noticed all three were really pointing toward the same idea.

Most of the valuable stuff inside a small business lives in one person's head. That's what makes it valuable, but it's also what makes it fragile.

When the room empties

A lot of the groups I work with only run well when one specific person is there. They're the individual, usually the founder, who knows how it all fits together. So the day they step out, traveling (or sick or laid up on crutches at a beach house), the whole thing idles until they're back.

It works despite those pauses for a while. But it's a nervous way to run, and most founders can feel it's a matter of time before a pause lands somewhere it can't afford to.

A question worth sitting with: If you couldn't show up for two weeks, no phone, no laptop, what would stop moving? Not slow down. Stop. That list is the map. Every item on it is a place where the business is really just you.

Getting it out of your head

So how do you actually do that? A client from this past year comes to mind.

They're a golf fitness brand, built over a decade. A serious operation -- 600+ videos, hundreds of workouts, a big engaged audience, real revenue. The method that made it work was genuinely good. It just lived mostly in the founder's head and scattered across a library nobody could fully see.

We got it out, and the process was less mysterious than it sounds. We indexed everything they already had. Then a standing weekly call where I'd ask questions and let him talk, and we'd get the method down on paper, tagged, and organized into a knowledge base.

It doesn't sound fancy. Just an hour where the goal was to move what he knew from his head into a place other people could reach.

Nobody writes a manual on their own time. But most people will happily answer questions about the thing they're best at.

That's the trick. You talk, someone else captures. Once it reached critical mass, we could shape it into a certification with course modules, all drawing from that one source.

If I were starting this in your business, I'd start with the messiest thing that only you can do. Extract that piece from your head, build the process, and move onto the next.

Why it's worth the trouble

Here's the part that ties it together, and it comes from a mentor of mine, Ken.

The best sales advice he ever gave me was to stop selling. He gets on a call and doesn't pitch or talk price. He digs into your problem until he understands it, and starts helping right there. His logic is that he wants to be sure he can add real value before he asks for anything. By the time cost comes up, the trust is already built.

I've been trying to run more like that. Get in, look around, be useful first, tell you the truth about what I see, even when the truth is "you don't need me for this."

And here's the connection I didn't see until I lined these up. When everything's trapped in your head, you're rationing yourself. When it's out where you can hand pieces of it over, being generous gets easy. Getting the knowledge out of your head is what makes being useful-first even possible.
